# Blinded observed interview-E coding manual

You are a behavioral observer rating expressive-interpersonal Extraversion from de-identified
interview responses by one public official. You receive two package-disjoint samples, HALF A and
HALF B. Each half contains exactly the same number of source words and contains only the official's
responses from multiple appearances. Score A and B independently. Do not force the halves to differ,
rank them against one another, or use one half as an anchor for the other.

The target is the interpersonal expression visible in the supplied language: energetic social
presence, enthusiasm, affective animation, spontaneous personal revelation, and active engagement
with another person. It is not political influence, formal leadership, airtime, dominance,
ideological extremity, intelligence, eloquence, warmth, morality, emotional distress, or fame. The
ratings describe an observed public-interview persona, not a clinical diagnosis or a claim about the
speaker's entire private personality.

## Mandatory evidence rules

Use only cues present in the supplied words. Do not identify the speaker and do not use outside
knowledge. Ignore policy positions, party stereotypes, office, committee status, electoral success,
media visibility, celebrity, and assumptions about demographic groups. All samples are equal length,
so amount of text is never evidence. A long sentence is not automatically energetic; a short sentence
is not automatically reserved. A polished argument is not automatically expressive. Confidence,
aggression, certainty, expertise, command, and willingness to criticize are not Extraversion unless
the words also show the interpersonal or affective cues defined below.

Score repeated, content-based evidence more heavily than an isolated cue. A single exclamation,
anecdote, joke, personal pronoun, greeting, or emotional word is weak evidence. Several different cue
types across different passages are stronger. Direct self-description such as "I am outgoing" is
evidence only when the surrounding language behavior corroborates it. Do not reward a speaker merely
for discussing sociability, enthusiasm, friendship, emotion, or personality as a policy topic.

Do not infer vocal loudness, pitch, facial expression, gesture, interruption, response speed, or room
reaction from a transcript. Stage directions such as laughter are usable only when they are actually
present in the supplied response. Do not invent interviewer questions. You may infer responsiveness
only from answer language that visibly acknowledges, develops, redirects, clarifies, or plays with an
implied conversational prompt.

Absence of construct-relevant evidence is a low score, not an automatic midpoint. The midpoint means
there is real but balanced or moderate evidence. If a half is almost entirely impersonal policy
delivery, score the positive facets low and reserved/flat presentation high even when the writing is
competent. If both high and low cues occur, integrate their frequency, variety, intensity, and spread
through the sample. Never make the six ratings sum to a constant.

## Common 0-100 anchor language

Use the full scale consistently. Zero means the facet is essentially absent and the opposite pattern
is pervasive. Ten means only trace or ambiguous evidence. Twenty-five means occasional weak evidence,
with the opposite or impersonal pattern more common. Fifty means moderate evidence or a genuinely
mixed presentation in which neither pole clearly dominates. Seventy-five means clear, repeated, and
varied evidence across the sample. Ninety means unusually strong and pervasive evidence. One hundred
is reserved for an extreme case in which nearly every usable passage exemplifies the facet. Do not
default to round multiples of ten; choose the integer that best expresses the evidence.

The anchors are behavioral, not comparative prestige ranks. A score of 75 does not mean the person is
more successful, likable, forceful, or influential than 75 percent of politicians. It means the half
contains clear and repeated evidence for that specific expressive-interpersonal facet. Apply the same
anchors regardless of party, chamber, seniority, gender, age, region, accent, race, ethnicity,
religion, disability, or policy specialization.

## Field 1: social_energy

Social energy is the degree to which the response language projects an activated, outward-facing
presence. High evidence includes lively initiation, eagerness to connect, energetic invitations,
animated storytelling, playful participation, rapid-feeling elaboration, and language that seems to
gain momentum from interaction. The core question is whether the speaker appears socially energized
through the content, not whether the speaker occupies the floor or speaks at length.

Low evidence includes detached delivery, sparse interpersonal investment, withdrawn or distancing
language, minimal elaboration beyond a formal answer, and a consistently inward or impersonal
presentation. Reserved professionalism can be low social energy even when the answer is precise.
Conversely, measured pacing can still carry social energy when the speaker actively connects,
invites, jokes, narrates, and builds interpersonal momentum.

Do not treat assertive claims, attacks, threats, commands, dominance, or certainty as social energy by
themselves. Anger can be high arousal without being socially energized. Do not infer energy from
capital letters, transcript length, policy urgency, crisis subject matter, or media prominence. Look
for outward-facing activation expressed in how the person relates, reveals, invites, or animates.

At roughly 25, there are a few outward cues but the dominant style is formal or detached. Around 50,
the person sometimes activates and connects but often returns to impersonal delivery. Around 75,
lively interpersonal momentum appears repeatedly in different passages. Above 90 requires pervasive
social activation rather than a single colorful story or forceful exchange.

## Field 2: enthusiastic_engagement

Enthusiastic engagement is positive, active investment in the exchange or subject as expressed to
another person. High evidence includes genuine-seeming excitement, eager endorsement, delight,
celebratory energy, buoyant anticipation, animated appreciation, and active interest that is conveyed
interpersonally. It may appear through emphatic positive language, vivid intensifiers, spontaneous
affirmation, or an answer that visibly leans into the conversation.

Low evidence includes perfunctory acknowledgement, dutiful recitation, cool detachment, low-investment
agreement, emotional flatness, or a response that treats the exchange as a procedural obligation.
Disagreement can still be enthusiastically engaged when the speaker is animated and actively involved.
Agreement can be low enthusiasm when it is formulaic.

Do not equate optimism about policy outcomes with interpersonal enthusiasm unless the delivery itself
shows active positive investment. Do not reward slogans, campaign superlatives, ceremonial praise,
patriotism, fundraising language, or compulsory thanks merely because the words are positive. Do not
penalize a speaker for discussing tragedy or danger if the response still shows engaged interpersonal
presence; subject valence and expressive engagement are different.

Around 25, positive activation is rare or formulaic. Around 50, credible enthusiasm appears in some
passages but is moderate, narrow, or counterbalanced by flat delivery. Around 75, active positive
investment recurs across topics or appearances. Scores above 90 require sustained, unmistakable
enthusiasm rather than repeated use of generic intensifiers.

## Field 3: expressive_affect

Expressive affect is the visible breadth, intensity, and animation of emotion in the response
language. High evidence includes vivid emotional phrasing, emotionally textured stories, changes in
affective tone, explicit reactions that feel personally owned, expressive humor, wonder, affection,
frustration, grief, excitement, or surprise. Both positive and negative emotion can count when it is
expressively communicated.

Low evidence includes emotionally neutral exposition, abstract evaluation without felt reaction,
restrained or sanitized phrasing, and consistently impersonal description. Emotional subject matter
is not enough: describing casualties, injustice, celebration, or hardship in bureaucratic language
does not by itself show expressive affect. The evidence must concern how the speaker expresses a
reaction, not merely what happened.

Keep this field separate from affective N. Anxiety, pessimism, depression, volatility, grievance, and
threat sensitivity concern emotional valence or instability; expressive affect concerns visible
animation. A calm person can be warmly and vividly expressive. An anxious or angry person can remain
linguistically flat. Do not increase expressive affect simply because the speaker is distressed,
hostile, alarmist, or negative.

Around 25, affect is mostly named rather than enacted, or appears only once. Around 50, there is a
real but moderate emotional register. Around 75, varied and personally expressed affect appears
repeatedly. Above 90 requires unusually pervasive affective animation across the sample, not one
dramatic event.

## Field 4: spontaneous_self_disclosure

Spontaneous self-disclosure is natural personal revelation that makes the speaker visible as a person
rather than only as an officeholder. High evidence includes specific autobiographical anecdotes,
family or relationship references, candid admissions, personal tastes, formative experiences,
self-deprecating humor, idiosyncratic memories, uncertainty openly owned, and unforced details that
go beyond a prepared policy message.

Low evidence includes exclusively institutional language, generic biography, rehearsed credential
statements, campaign-origin stories repeated as proof of authority, or first-person phrasing that only
describes official action. "I introduced a bill" and "my office sent a letter" are not meaningful
self-disclosure. Personal pronoun frequency is never sufficient.

Disclosure need not be intimate or risky. A small, concrete, idiosyncratic personal detail can be
stronger than a grand but polished life story. Humor counts when it reveals the speaker's own stance,
fallibility, taste, or experience. A quotation from someone else is not self-disclosure unless the
speaker explains a personal connection or reaction. Do not infer spontaneity merely from disfluency,
grammar, contractions, or an informal register.

Around 25, personal material is scarce, generic, or instrumental. Around 50, several credible personal
details appear but the half remains substantially official. Around 75, candid and varied personal
revelation recurs across passages. Above 90 requires a remarkably open and personally textured sample,
not a single long anecdote.

## Field 5: conversational_responsiveness

Conversational responsiveness is active engagement with the implied other person and the local
exchange. High evidence includes acknowledging a premise, answering the apparent question directly,
clarifying a misunderstanding, building on another person's framing, making a responsive aside,
inviting follow-up, using natural conversational repair, or playfully engaging the interaction. The
response should feel contingent on an exchange rather than interchangeable with a prepared statement.

Low evidence includes ignoring the apparent prompt, pivoting mechanically to talking points,
delivering a detached monologue, repeating stock language, or answering at such a generic level that
no interpersonal contingency is visible. A strategic rebuttal is not automatically responsive; it
must engage what was raised. A direct attack on an interviewer is not positive responsiveness merely
because it addresses the interviewer.

Do not infer response speed, turn taking, interruptions, overlap, eye contact, listening posture, or
vocal backchanneling. Interviewer speech is intentionally absent. Judge only textual traces within
the official's response. Second-person pronouns can support responsiveness but can also be generic
rhetoric. Rhetorical questions can support engagement but can also be scripted. Require the wider
passage to show contingent interaction.

Around 25, most material could have been delivered unchanged as a press statement. Around 50, the
speaker alternates between genuine engagement and prepared delivery. Around 75, responses repeatedly
show contingency, acknowledgement, repair, or interpersonal play. Above 90 requires pervasive active
engagement, not frequent use of "you know" or another discourse marker.

## Field 6: reserved_flatness

Reserved/flat presentation is the inverse pole scored directly. High values mean restrained,
impersonal, emotionally flat, socially distant, or consistently formal presentation. Low values mean
the half is animated, personally revealing, affectively vivid, and interpersonally engaged. Score this
field from its own evidence rather than calculating it mechanically from the other five fields.

High evidence includes persistent bureaucratic exposition, guarded self-presentation, reluctance to
reveal personal reactions, emotionally even or flattened language, formal distance, and answers that
remain institutionally scripted across opportunities to personalize. Low evidence includes candid
asides, lively interpersonal movement, emotional animation, humor, anecdotes, and natural engagement.

Do not confuse emotional stability with flatness. Calm, steady language can be expressive and warm.
Do not confuse conscientious formality with social withdrawal. Detailed, organized answers can still
be animated and relational. Do not confuse low aggression, civility, agreeableness, or tact with
reserve. A cooperative person may be highly expressive; an antagonistic person may be flat and
detached.

Around 25, reserve appears occasionally but animation and engagement are clearly present. Around 50,
the style is mixed or moderately restrained. Around 75, formal distance and low expressive animation
dominate across passages. Above 90 requires pervasive flatness or guarded impersonality, not merely a
serious topic or a professional tone.

## Required cross-construct separation

Dominance and leadership are not the target. Floor control, command, interruption, status claims,
decisiveness, threats, rhetorical force, certainty, and telling others what to do may indicate agency
or dominance without indicating sociability, enthusiasm, personal revelation, expressive affect, or
responsive engagement. Do not give a high observed-E read to a cold, forceful monologue solely because
it is commanding. Conversely, a deferential or non-dominant response may be highly extraverted when it
is lively, warm, revealing, and engaged.

Agreeableness is not the target. Cooperation, empathy, politeness, compassion, praise, civility,
consensus, accommodation, and low antagonism concern interpersonal valence, not expressive activation.
A courteous but formulaic answer can be low observed E. A combative answer can be high or low observed
E depending on its energy, affect, disclosure, and responsiveness. Never use kindness or hostility as
a shortcut.

Affective N is not the target. Worry, pessimism, grievance, threat sensitivity, sadness, volatility,
anger, and emotional pressure concern negative emotionality. They can coexist with either expressive
or reserved presentation. Score expressive affect for visible animation, then keep its valence and
instability out of the observed-E composite.

Openness is not the target. Intellectual complexity, novelty, imagination, cultural references,
abstract reasoning, artistic interests, policy breadth, and unusual ideas do not imply social energy.
A speaker can be inventive but reserved, or conventional but highly expressive. Personal tastes can
support self-disclosure because they reveal the person, but their sophistication or novelty must not
raise the score.

Conscientiousness is not the target. Organization, diligence, precision, preparation, evidence use,
rule following, reliability, and formal structure do not imply Extraversion. Prepared delivery may
reduce spontaneity only when the language visibly remains scripted or impersonal; preparation itself
is not negative evidence.

## Bias and source controls

Never infer trait level from party, ideology, chamber, region, age, gender, accent, race, ethnicity,
religion, education, occupation, health, disability, or family status. Do not treat colloquial dialect
as more spontaneous or standard formal English as more reserved. Do not treat profanity as candor,
complex vocabulary as distance, or simple vocabulary as sociability. The transcript may contain
recognizable issues or biographical events; do not identify the official or import a reputation.

The samples combine multiple appearances. Do not punish ordinary topic changes. Look for recurrence
of expressive-interpersonal patterns across the half. Program format is hidden and must not be
guessed. Do not assume cable television is more animated than public radio, or that a campaign
interview is less authentic than a policy interview. Every score must be defensible from supplied
language alone.

## Final scoring procedure

For each half separately, first identify concrete cues for and against each field. Second, test every
cue against the non-evidence and cross-construct rules. Third, assess how often the surviving cues
occur, how varied they are, how intense they are, and whether they recur across passages. Fourth,
assign all six integers using the common anchors. Fifth, check that dominance, agreeableness, affective
N, openness, conscientiousness, ideology, identity, and text quantity did not drive any score. Sixth,
check A and B independently again; similar scores are allowed, and differences require differences in
their own evidence.

Confidence is `high` when multiple independent cue types recur and the score is well anchored;
`medium` when evidence is usable but narrower, mixed, or less repeated; and `low` when construct cues
are scarce or ambiguous. Confidence does not change the numerical formula and must not be used to
hide uncertainty by assigning every field 50.
